Most countries have one favourite place to send their students. I coloured every country by where its own students most often go to university abroad, every year from {{meta.first_year|raw}} to {{meta.last_year|raw}}. Old empires still show through, Turkey has become a magnet, and India has overtaken China in Britain.

Where would you go to university if you left home? For a student in Lagos the answer is very likely Britain. In Almaty it is Russia, in Dakar it is France, in Hanoi it is now Japan. The reasons are old and familiar: a shared language, a shared history, an exam system built on the same model, and cousins who went before. Add them up across a whole country and you get a map with surprisingly sharp edges.

The UNESCO Institute for Statistics collects, from almost every country, the number of international students in its universities and where they come from. Turn that table round and it tells you where each country's students go. In {{meta.last_year|raw}} it counts {{v.students_counted_last|int}} students abroad in {{meta.hosts_last|int}} host countries. For every country and every year I picked the single most common destination, and coloured the map by it.

The map

The United States is still the most common answer: it is number one for {{v.n_usa_last|int}} countries in {{meta.last_year|raw}}. Then come the United Kingdom ({{v.n_gbr_last|int}}), France ({{v.n_fra_last|int}}), Australia ({{v.n_aus_last|int}}) and Germany ({{v.n_deu_last|int}}). Below them is a list that tells you a lot about neighbours. Argentina is first for {{top_counts.2023.ARG|int}} countries, including Brazil, Peru and Colombia. South Africa is first for much of southern Africa. Germans go to Austria and Austrians go to Germany.

Press play on the interactive version and the map changes slowly. In {{meta.first_year|raw}} the United States led {{v.n_usa_first|int}} countries. Some of that fall is real and some is a quirk of the data, because Germany did not report where its students came from until {{v.deu_first_year|raw}}. Counting only countries measured cleanly in both years, the United States went from {{changed_2013.count_a.USA|int}} to {{changed_2013.count_b.USA|int}} between 2013 and {{meta.last_year|raw}}, with Australia the most common winner.

Empires and languages

The strongest patterns are older than any student on the map. Russia is number one for all five Central Asian republics, and {{v.rus_ca_last|pct}} of their students abroad are in Russia. Portugal takes {{v.prt_luso_last|pct}} of students from Angola, Mozambique and the other Portuguese-speaking countries of Africa. France is first for most of French-speaking Africa.

But look at the shares and the old routes are loosening. In {{meta.first_year|raw}}, {{v.fra_franco_first|pct}} of students abroad from French-speaking sub-Saharan Africa were in France. By {{meta.last_year|raw}} it was {{v.fra_franco_last|pct}}. Senegal and Morocco have become regional hubs, and Canada and Turkey have taken a share too. Senegal is now first for {{v.sen_tops_last|int}} of its neighbours. The exception runs the other way: Britain's share of students from English-speaking Africa rose from {{v.gbr_anglo_first|pct}} to {{v.gbr_anglo_last|pct}}, almost all of it after 2020, as Nigerians poured into British universities.

The new magnets

The biggest surprise for me was Turkey. It had {{magnets.TUR.v0|int}} international students in {{magnets.TUR.y0|raw}} and {{magnets.TUR.v1|int}} in {{magnets.TUR.y1|raw}}, a rise of {{magnets.TUR.x|f1}} times. They come from its neighbours and its language family: Syria, Azerbaijan, Iran and Turkmenistan send the most, and Turkey is now the number one destination for Syria, Azerbaijan and Iran. South Korea grew {{magnets.KOR.x|f1}} times, mostly with students from China and Vietnam, and is first for Mongolia. Malaysia grew {{magnets.MYS.x|f1}} times. And Vietnam, whose top destination was the United States until 2016, now sends more students to Japan and to South Korea.

That is the main hole in the map. The Emirates reported {{magnets.ARE.v1|int}} international students in {{magnets.ARE.y1|raw}} and China {{v.chn_inbound_last|int}}, but neither gives a breakdown, so neither can be anyone's number one here. Where a missing host could plausibly be first, I draw the country with a dashed outline rather than pretend to know.

India, China and Britain

China is still the world's biggest sender: {{v.chn_out_last|int}} students abroad in {{meta.last_year|raw}} on UNESCO's estimate, against {{v.ind_out_last|int}} from India. But India's numbers jumped after 2019 while China's flattened, and in Britain India has already overtaken China. HESA, which counts students at every UK university, shows India passing China in {{uk.india_over_china_first_year|raw}}. In {{uk.last_year|raw}} there were {{uk.top_last.0.1|int}} students from India and {{uk.top_last.1.1|int}} from China; ten years earlier India sent just {{uk.top_first.1.1|int}}.

The interactive version has a map for every UK university. The change shows up there too: among universities and colleges with at least 500 international students, China was the biggest source at {{uk.provider_top_counts_2015.China|int}} in {{uk.first_year|raw}} and India at {{uk.provider_top_counts_2015.India|int}}. By {{uk.last_year|raw}} it was {{uk.provider_top_counts_2025.China|int}} for China and {{uk.provider_top_counts_2025.India|int}} for India. Meanwhile students from France, Italy and Germany all fell after Brexit.

How I did this

The data are UNESCO's counts of internationally mobile tertiary students by host country and country of origin, retrieved from the UIS data API. Many countries skip years, so I filled a gap only when there were reported years on both sides (by a straight line) or carried the nearest year over for at most two years. Of {{meta.filled_rows|int}} rows used, {{meta.filled_by_method.interpolated|int}} are interpolated and {{meta.filled_by_method.carried|int}} carried. Countries with fewer than 50 students counted abroad are not ranked. Where a large host with no breakdown that year could be first, the country is flagged as uncertain ({{v.unc_last|int}} countries in {{meta.last_year|raw}}).

I checked the table two ways. The OECD's shares of each country's students by destination match the UNESCO-based shares to within one percentage point {{checks.oecd_within_1pp|pct}} of the time, and UNESCO's total for the UK in {{v.uk_check_year|raw}} is within {{v.uk_check_diff|pct1}} of HESA's own count. The main limits: hosts that never report origins (China, Singapore, Lebanon) cannot be ranked, Japan reports only part of its students by origin from 2015, and definitions of "mobile" student differ a little between countries.
